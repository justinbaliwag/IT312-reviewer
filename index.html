Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

5 Core Phases

The SDLC is an organized process for planning, building, checking, and maintaining a software system. It helps a team move from an idea to a working product in manageable steps.

Phase In Simple Words Everyday Example (Queue System)
Requirements Find out what users need and what the system must do. A school asks for an online queue system and explains who can get a number and what information is needed.
Design Plan how the system will look and work before building it. The team sketches the queue screen and plans how student records will be stored.
Implementation Write the code and build the planned features. Developers create the queue-number form and the staff dashboard.
Integration & Troubleshooting Connect separate parts and fix problems between them. The queue form submits a number, but the dashboard does not show it; the team checks the connection.
Testing Check whether the system works as expected and find defects. Students try to get queue numbers, and testers check invalid inputs and duplicate requests.
Asking users? Requirements
Planning screens/structure? Design
Writing feature code? Implementation
Connecting modules? Integration & Troubleshooting
Verifying behavior/defects? Testing

* Remember: A real project may revisit earlier phases. The SDLC is a guide, not always a one-way checklist.

Technical Reviews and Audits

7 Formal Checkpoints

A review or audit is a structured check used to find issues, confirm readiness, or verify that a software product matches its agreed requirements and records. The key is to identify what is being checked and when.

Review / Audit Definition (What is checked) Real-World Example
Requirements Review Checks whether requirements are clear, complete, consistent, and understandable before committing to them. A team notices that "fast loading" has no measurable target and asks the client to define an acceptable loading time.
Peer / Incremental Review A colleague checks a small part of the work as it is developed, rather than waiting until the whole product is finished. A developer asks a teammate to inspect a newly added password-reset feature before merging it.
Critical Design Review (CDR) A detailed check of the proposed design to see whether it can meet requirements and is ready to proceed. Before building a payment feature, the team reviews the planned data flow, security controls, and failure handling.
Test Readiness Review (TRR) Checks whether system, test cases, tools, environment, and people are ready for formal testing. The team confirms that test accounts and sample data are available before running a full test cycle.
First Article Inspection (FAI) Inspects the first completed item or implementation to verify that it matches the specified design or requirements. The first manufactured device is checked against drawings; in software, an initial deliverable is checked against its specification.
Functional Configuration Audit (FCA) Verifies that the system's actual functions and performance satisfy the approved functional requirements. Auditors run defined scenarios to confirm that a booking system performs the actions promised in its requirements.
Physical Configuration Audit (PCA) Checks that the delivered product and its documentation match the approved configuration and records. The team compares the release version, installed components, and accompanying documents against the approved configuration list.

Fast Comparison Clues:

• Requirements Review: Are requirements good enough to work from?
• Peer/Incremental: Catch small issues while still being developed?
• CDR: Is the design sound and ready to move forward?
• TRR: Are we ready to test (env, tools, data)?
• FAI: Does the first completed item match what was specified?
• FCA: Does the product perform the required functions?
• PCA: Does delivered configuration match approved records?

Types of Software Requirements

3-Part Classification

A requirement describes something a system must do or a condition it must satisfy. Requirements help users and developers agree on what "done correctly" means.

1. Functional

Describes a service, action, or behavior the system must provide.

Example: A student portal must let a student view their class schedule.
2. Non-Functional

Describes a quality, constraint, or standard for how the system operates.

Example: The schedule page should load within two seconds under normal conditions.
3. Domain Requirement

Comes from rules, practices, or constraints specific to the field where used.

Example: A university system must follow the institution's approved grading rules.

Requirements Development Activities

5 Iterative Activities

The work of discovering, understanding, documenting, and checking what stakeholders need from a system.

Ask (Elicitation)
Sort (Analysis & Negotiation)
Write (Specification)
Confirm (Validation)
Track (Management)
Activity What Happens Example (Enrollment Process)
Elicitation Gather needs from users and stakeholders. Interview students and staff about problems with the current enrollment process.
Analysis & Negotiation Clarify, compare, prioritize, and resolve conflicting needs. Students want many optional fields, but staff need a short form; the team agrees which fields are essential.
Specification Write the agreed requirements clearly so they can be understood and checked. Document the fields, permissions, and expected behavior of the enrollment form.
Validation Confirm that documented requirements reflect real needs and are feasible and testable. Show the written requirements to staff and ask them to confirm that no important enrollment rule is missing.
Requirements Management Track changes to requirements and their impact over time. When the university changes an enrollment rule, the team records the change and identifies affected features and tests.

Use Case Diagrams

High-Level Behavioral View

A use case diagram gives a high-level picture of who interacts with a system and what goals they can achieve. It shows system behavior from the user's point of view, not the internal code or step-by-step screen layout.

Actor

A person or external system that interacts with the system.

Ex: Student, Librarian, Payment Service
Use Case

A goal or service the system provides; drawn as an oval.

Ex: Borrow a book, Pay a fee, View schedule
System Boundary

A box that defines what belongs to the system being modeled.

Ex: Box labeled "Campus Library System"
Association

A line showing that an actor participates in a use case.

Ex: Student line to "Search Catalog"
«include»

Shows required behavior reused by another use case.

Ex: "Borrow Book" includes "Check Borrowing Eligibility"
«extend»

Shows optional or conditional behavior added to a base use case.

Ex: "Apply Discount" extends "Checkout" when qualifying

How to turn a paragraph into a use case diagram (7 Steps):

  1. Identify the system being described and draw a labeled boundary box.
  2. Underline the people or external systems that interact with it; these are possible actors.
  3. Look for goal-oriented verbs such as search, register, update, pay, cancel, or view.
  4. Turn each valid system goal into a use case, usually written as a short verb phrase.
  5. Connect each actor to the use cases they participate in.
  6. Add include or extend relationships only when the described behavior supports them; do not add them just to make the diagram look complex.
  7. Check that every use case is inside the system boundary and actors remain outside it.

Essay Challenge 1 • Technical Audits

Distinguishing FCA vs. PCA in Architecture Deliverables

Explain the core difference between a Functional Configuration Audit (FCA) and a Physical Configuration Audit (PCA). Provide a realistic software system integration scenario illustrating when each audit is triggered.

1. Standard Model Answer (Textbook Explanation)

FCA (Functional Configuration Audit) verifies that the system's actual functions and performance satisfy the approved functional requirements. (E.g., Auditors run defined scenarios to confirm that a booking system performs the actions promised in its requirements).
PCA (Physical Configuration Audit) checks that the delivered product and its documentation match the approved configuration and records. (E.g., The team compares the release version, installed components, and accompanying documents against the approved configuration list).

2. Key Points Rubric (Did your answer include these?)

  • Stated that FCA focuses on functional performance and scenario execution against specs.
  • Stated that PCA focuses on version numbers, physical binaries, libraries, and matching documentation.
  • Recognized that FCA asks: "Does it do what was promised?" while PCA asks: "Does what is packaged match the approved inventory records?"

3. Practical Business & Architecture Implication

Failing an FCA results in software defects and functional failure for users. Failing a PCA results in deployment failure, broken dependencies in production, licensing/compliance risks, and unmaintainable legacy releases where no one knows which source code built the binary.

Essay Challenge 2 • SDLC

The Critical Role of Integration & Troubleshooting

Why is "Integration & Troubleshooting" distinct from "Implementation" and "Testing"? Explain what happens during this phase and what clue distinguishes it in an exam scenario.

1. Standard Model Answer (Textbook Explanation)

Implementation is writing the code and building the isolated features. Integration & Troubleshooting is connecting separate parts and fixing communication problems between them (e.g., a queue form submits a number, but the dashboard does not show it; the team checks the connection). Testing checks whether the complete system works as expected and uncovers defects against user requirements.

2. Key Points Rubric (Did your answer include these?)

  • Distinguished building individual features (Implementation) from connecting them (Integration).
  • Identified communication/middleware breakdown between components as the core trigger.
  • Used the PDF's distinction clue: Connecting modules or fixing communication between them? Integration and troubleshooting.

3. Practical Business & Architecture Implication

In enterprise architectures, microservices or external APIs often function perfectly in unit tests (Implementation), but fail when integrated due to network timeouts, payload mismatches, authentication tokens, or CORS errors. Integration is where architectural interfaces are validated.

Essay Challenge 3 • Requirements Engineering

Elicitation vs. Analysis & Negotiation

Suppose two department heads disagree about who can approve student loan exemptions in an ERP system. Which requirements activity addresses this, what must happen before writing formal specifications, and why is this not merely "Elicitation"?

1. Standard Model Answer (Textbook Explanation)

This falls under Analysis and Negotiation. Elicitation is simply gathering needs (interviews/workshops). When needs conflict or overlap (e.g., departments disagree on approval authority), the team must clarify, compare, prioritize, and negotiate an agreed-upon business rule before documenting it in the specification.

2. Key Points Rubric (Did your answer include these?)

  • Correctly named Analysis and Negotiation.
  • Explicitly stated that Elicitation is only gathering/discovering information, not resolving disagreements.
  • Mentioned the sequence: Ask (Elicit) → Sort/Negotiate → Write (Specify) → Confirm (Validate) → Track.

3. Practical Business & Architecture Implication

Writing code or database permissions before negotiating governance causes massive political friction, scope creep, and expensive rework. System architects must ensure business rules have definitive ownership before technical implementation begins.

Essay Challenge 4 • UML & Use Case Modeling

Comparing «include» vs. «extend» Relationships

Clearly explain how to differentiate an «include» relationship from an «extend» relationship in a Use Case Diagram. Provide a distinct real-world software example for each.

1. Standard Model Answer (Textbook Explanation)

«include» shows required behavior reused by another use case; the base use case cannot be considered complete without executing the included step (e.g., "Borrow Book" includes "Check Borrowing Eligibility").
«extend» shows optional or conditional behavior added to a base use case only under specific circumstances (e.g., "Apply Discount" extends "Checkout" only when the user qualifies).

2. Key Points Rubric (Did your answer include these?)

  • Stated that «include» is mandatory / always required.
  • Stated that «extend» is conditional / optional.
  • Gave concrete, valid examples for both.
  • Noted rule 6: Do not add them just to make the diagram look complex.

3. Practical Business & Architecture Implication

Using «include» properly enables modular architectural reuse (such as shared identity or credit verification services). Using «extend» allows core system flows to remain clean without being cluttered by edge-case business logic.
